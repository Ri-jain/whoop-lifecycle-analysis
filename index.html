01 · Where this started

How I came to WHOOP

I have worn a fitness tracker since I was 15. Over the years I cycled through most of them, several versions of Fitbit, a couple of Apple Watches, and eventually WHOOP. WHOOP was the one that stood out. Its insights into recovery, strain, and sleep were sharper and more useful than anything I had seen before, and for the first time the data actually changed how I trained and rested.

My WHOOP journey began on a free trial in late 2025, as a student. At the trial-to-paid step I chose the monthly plan, but the checkout moved to charge the full annual amount; my account balance stopped the transaction, and I never converted to a paying member. Two things discouraged me, and they were separate: the checkout defaulting to the larger commitment at the moment I was deciding, and, even if it had worked, a full price I could not have carried on a student budget. Neither was about the product, which I loved.

That gap, between wanting the product and being able to stay, stayed with me. When I saw the open role on the Lifecycle Marketing team, I wanted to explore the problem more deeply and to see how I might help. So I started with the public record.

02 · What the reviews say

A beloved product, a strained relationship

The pattern is consistent: members praise the product and resent the relationship, and the resentment concentrates at a few specific moments, cancellation, renewal, and hardware upgrades. A quieter theme is a sense of diminishing returns (about 1% of reviews, on every platform): members feel they are seeing the same insights and start doing the math on cost. And when members name a rival, it is Garmin far more than Oura (459 mentions versus 123), which suggests the threat they feel is the subscription itself.

03 · The tests

Two experiments at the moments that hurt

The reviews point at specific, controllable moments. Both tests below target one of them, both are grounded in a measured theme, and both name a risk, because a test that watches only its target metric is not a test.

Test A · cancellation · retention

Cancel-flow save offers

Evidence
Cancellation friction (3.9%) and subscription resentment (6.7%) are top leave-themes, and the cancel flow is where the most frustrated members already are. Today, if they exit, they cancel; there is no save.
Mechanism
Intercept members who initiate the cancel flow with a segmented offer: three months at a reduced price, a pause option (skip 60 days without losing history), or a routed "why are you leaving?" survey.
Hypothesis
Cancel-flow save rate is a heavily underinvested metric. Most companies lose members who could have been kept for pennies.
Measure
Cancel-flow save rate and 90-day retention of saved members.
Risk
Some members should simply be let go; false-positive saves cost support time without producing loyal renewers.
Test B · hardware release · retention and trust

Pre-emptive upgrade clarity email

Evidence
The WHOOP 5.0 upgrade-fee backlash is a measured theme (4.3% of reviews), and the complaints read as surprise, not sticker price.
Mechanism
Members six or more months into their subscription, sent ahead of any next hardware release. Two variants: one proactively explains exactly what will happen at the release (pricing, eligibility, timing); the other is the status quo.
Hypothesis
The 5.0 backlash was surprise more than price. Surprise avoided is anger avoided.
Measure
NPS delta on the upgrade communication and cancel-rate delta versus prior release windows.
Risk
Telegraphing an unfavorable change could prompt some members to cancel earlier; watch net renewal revenue across the window.

From measurement to member experience

Each test turns what members feel into what WHOOP says. The loyalty themes decide what to amplify: lead lifecycle messaging with sleep, recovery, and behavior-change value, because those are the measured sources of stickiness. The leave-themes decide what to preempt: a save offer at cancellation, clarity ahead of an upgrade, transparency before a renewal. The model scores the member, the score picks the message, the experiment measures the lift, and the result retrains the model.

The product is already loved. What the reviews describe is a relationship that frays at a few controllable moments, and each of those moments is a message WHOOP owns. That is not a product problem. It is a lifecycle problem, which is the kind this team exists to solve.
