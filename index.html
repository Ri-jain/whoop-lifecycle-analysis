01 · Background

I have worn a fitness tracker since I was 15. Over the years I cycled through most of them, several versions of Fitbit, a couple of Apple Watches, and eventually WHOOP. WHOOP was the one that stood out. Its insights into recovery, strain, and sleep were sharper and more useful than anything I had seen before, and for the first time the data actually changed how I trained and rested.

I started on a free trial in late 2025, as a student. When the trial ended I chose the monthly plan, but the checkout moved to charge the full annual amount, and my account balance stopped the transaction. I never became a paying member. Two separate things discouraged me. First, the checkout defaulted to the larger commitment at the moment I was deciding. Second, even if the charge had gone through, the price was more than I could carry on a student budget. Neither was about the product, which I loved.

That gap between wanting the product and being able to afford it stuck with me. When I saw the open role on the Lifecycle Marketing team, I wanted to dig into the problem and see how I might help. So I started with the public record.

The pattern is consistent: members praise the product and resent the relationship, and the resentment concentrates at a few specific moments: cancellation, renewal, and hardware upgrades. A quieter theme is a sense of diminishing returns (about 1% of reviews, on every platform): members feel they are seeing the same insights and start doing the math on cost. And when members name a rival, it is Garmin far more than Oura (459 mentions versus 123), which suggests the threat they feel is the subscription itself.

03 · Proposed experiments

The reviews point to specific, controllable moments. Each test below targets one of them, is grounded in a measured theme, and names its own risk, because a test that tracks only its target metric can miss the harm it causes elsewhere.

Test A · trial-to-paid · conversion

Term choice at the trial-to-paid step

Evidence
Subscription resentment (6.7%) and price and long-run cost (5.7%) are top leave-themes, and both concentrate at the trial-to-paid step, where a trial member is asked to commit to a full year at once. It is also the exact moment I dropped off: the checkout moved to the annual amount as I was deciding.
Mechanism
At the trial-to-paid step, present three explicit terms (3, 6, and 12 months), priced so the longer commitments carry a clear per-month discount. Do not pre-select the largest; let the member choose the commitment they can actually afford.
Hypothesis
A lower-commitment entry point converts trial members who balk at a full year, and a share of them step up to a longer term at renewal once the product has proven itself.
Measure
Trial-to-paid conversion rate, and blended 12-month revenue per trial, so a lift in conversions is not bought by cannibalizing annual commitments.
Risk
Cheaper short terms could pull down members who would have committed annually anyway; watch revenue per trial, not conversion count alone.
Test B · cancellation · retention

Cancel-flow save offers

Evidence
Cancellation friction (3.9%) and subscription resentment (6.7%) are top leave-themes, and the cancel flow is where the most frustrated members already are. Today, if they exit, they cancel; there is no save.
Mechanism
Intercept members who initiate the cancel flow with a segmented offer: three months at a reduced price, a pause option (skip 60 days without losing history), or a routed "why are you leaving?" survey.
Hypothesis
Cancel-flow save rate is a heavily underinvested metric. Most companies lose members who could have been kept for pennies.
Measure
Cancel-flow save rate and 90-day retention of saved members.
Risk
Some members should simply be let go; false-positive saves cost support time without producing loyal renewers.
Test C · hardware release · retention and trust

Pre-emptive upgrade clarity email

Evidence
The WHOOP 5.0 upgrade-fee backlash is a measured theme (4.3% of reviews), and the complaints read as surprise, not sticker price.
Mechanism
Target members six or more months into their subscription, reached before the next hardware release. Two variants: one explains exactly what will happen at the release (pricing, eligibility, timing); the other is the status quo.
Hypothesis
The 5.0 backlash was about surprise more than price, so warning members ahead of time should prevent most of the anger.
Measure
NPS delta on the upgrade communication and cancel-rate delta versus prior release windows.
Risk
Telegraphing an unfavorable change could prompt some members to cancel earlier; watch net renewal revenue across the window.

From measurement to member experience

The analysis maps directly onto messaging. The loyalty themes set what to amplify: lifecycle messaging should lead with sleep, recovery, and behavior change, the value members most often credit for staying. The leave-themes set what to preempt: a save offer at cancellation, clear terms before a hardware upgrade, and advance notice before a renewal. In practice this runs as a closed loop. A model scores each member, the score selects the message, an experiment measures the lift, and the results feed back into the model.

04 · Member communications

The strategy ultimately lives in the messages members receive. The three samples below, one for each money moment, apply the same principles: open with the member rather than the ask, state the date and amount before any charge, and keep the cancel path clearly visible. Each maps to one of the tests above.

Trial → paid · Test A

SubjectYour trial ends in 3 days: pick the term that fits

Your free trial wraps up soon. When you're ready, choose the commitment that works for you:

3 months: try it season to season 6 months: most popular 12 months: best per-month value

No plan is pre-selected, and you'll see the exact amount and date before anything is charged.

Pre-renewal · billing transparency

SubjectHeads up: your renewal is July 30 ($199)

This is a reminder, not a charge yet. On July 30 your annual membership renews at $199. Nothing to do if you're staying. This is just so it's never a surprise.

Want to change your plan, pause, or cancel first? All three take about 30 seconds.

Cancellation · Test B

SubjectBefore you go, a couple of options

We saw you started to cancel. No hard sell, but two things worth knowing first:

Pause instead: step away up to 60 days and keep all your history and trends. 3 months at a lower rate, if cost is the reason.

And if it's simply time to move on, you can cancel in one tap and keep your data export, with no maze to click through.

The product is already loved. What the reviews describe is a relationship that frays at a few controllable moments, and each of those moments is a message WHOOP owns. That is not a product problem. It is a lifecycle problem, which is the kind this team exists to solve.
